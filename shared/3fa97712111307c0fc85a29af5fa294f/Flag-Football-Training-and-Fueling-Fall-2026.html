More skilled, not more exhausted.

Updated practice context: Alina practices at school 5:30–7:30 a.m. most schooldays. Use the after-school and weekend family guide for optional extras. This page's larger Session A/B blocks are references to integrate within or replace school work with coach approval, not additional home workouts.

This is an educational starter plan, not medical clearance or an individualized prescription. Her injury history, current workload, medical conditions, dietary needs, and actual game calendar are unknown. Have Alina, her parent, coach, and athletic trainer review it together before adding work. A pediatric clinician or sports dietitian should personalize medical and nutrition concerns.

The most important scheduling rule: use these blocks within or instead of comparable team work. Do not stack the whole plan on top of full practices, team lifting, conditioning, and games. If the coach already covers an exercise or skill, that work counts. New strength work requires qualified supervision. [H1, H2]

Ready to train

No pain, normal energy, adequately fed and hydrated, reasonable sleep, and coach-approved workload. Start with week 1 even if she is already good at catching.

Reduce or skip extras

Poor sleep, heavy legs, unusual soreness, exam stress, very hot conditions, a hard team practice, or another game within 48 hours: omit speed, hard cuts, and lower-body lifting. Choose brief easy catches or rest.

Stop and get help

Sharp pain, limping, joint swelling, dizziness, chest pain, fainting, or symptoms after a head hit: stop and tell an adult/athletic trainer. Suspected concussion means no same-day return and medical evaluation. Confusion or collapse in the heat is a 911 emergency. [H3, H4]

Protect the basics

At least one full day each week without organized training; aim for 8–10 hours of sleep. No punishment runs, fasted workouts, weight-loss targets, or supplement-driven shortcuts. [H1, H2, H5]

Equipment: her game ball and legal quick-release belt, a reliable thrower, 4–6 cones or flat markers, water, shade, supportive sports shoes/approved cleats, and an even, clear surface. Bands or light dumbbells are optional for supervised strength. Use indoor space when conditions require it.

Two-game weeks, tournaments, and playoffs

Games replace hard sessions: omit A/B extra speed, cutting, and lower-body lifting. If fresh and the coach agrees, use 5–10 minutes of easy catches or walkthroughs on a non-game day, never as a makeup workout. Keep a full rest day when the team calendar permits; if the school schedule leaves none, discuss workload with the coach and trainer. Within 48 hours of an important game, skip testing and unfamiliar exercise. After the season, redesign the plan rather than repeating this in-season load indefinitely.

Short accelerations Session A

Setup: markers at 0, 5, and 10 yards plus at least 10 clear yards to slow down. Use the stance her coach wants, not an improvised illegal rush alignment.

Do: on a partner's visual ball-movement cue, push the ground back and take quick first steps. Let the torso rise naturally; keep eyes up. Follow the week's 4-rep prescription. Recover 60–90 seconds between efforts, longer if not ready.

Feel: crisp and fast but relaxed, never straining. Stop if she is stumbling, overstriding, slowing noticeably, or feels hamstring/calf pain. These are speed reps, not repeated sprints to exhaustion.

Cut, plant, and stick Session B

Setup: run-in marker 5 yards away; two exit markers 3 yards to either side. Begin at a jog. Approach, shorten steps, lower hips, and make a controlled 45-degree cut, then stop balanced.

Dose: 2 reps each direction, 30–45 seconds rest. Keep the foot plant reasonably under the body, knee aligned with the foot, and trunk controlled. Learn braking before adding speed; do not chase an extreme cut angle.

Mirror the hips Session B

Setup: a 4 × 4-yard box, partners facing 2–3 yards apart. One moves side-to-side at controlled speed while Alina mirrors the hips without contact or crossing into the partner's space.

Dose: 2 × 10 seconds, 40–60 seconds rest. Start with one planned direction change, then progress to an unpredictable cue. Avoid standing tall, crossing feet in a short shuffle, or reaching with the upper body.

Not the priority: long conditioning runs, endless ladders, or high-volume shuttle tests. Ladders can warm up feet, but reacting to a partner and braking well transfer more directly to this plan's goals. This is a coaching choice, not a claim that one drill guarantees faster play.

Her answer to a double screen.

Arizona uses non-contact screen blocking. Blockers may use legal positioning, not contact, to obstruct. Once a legal screen is established, Alina must avoid it. Two screens are not automatically a penalty. Swims, clubs, rips, bull rushes, hand-fighting, and stiff-arms that contact opponents do not belong in this plan. [R1, R2]

A four-part rush decision

  1. Assignment: confirm inside rush versus outside containment and the legal pre-snap alignment with the coach. Watch the ball rather than guessing the cadence.
  2. Read space: identify the nearest open edge before getting boxed in. Do not aim at the bodies or try to squeeze a closed two-player gap.
  3. One move: sell a short inside step, then accelerate outside; or briefly change pace and redirect into the lane the screen leaves open. Keep enough room to avoid contact.
  4. Controlled finish: curve toward the QB's near hip, shorten steps, and attempt the flag. Do not overrun the QB, swing at her arm, or abandon contain for a low-percentage sack.

A good rep can be “no escape,” not “sack.” If two blockers occupy her, she may help the defense by keeping the QB inside or forcing an early throw. Have the coach grade assignment, pressure, and flag-pull opportunity—not sacks alone.

Two-screen read drill

Setup: two volunteers or two wide cone gates represent screeners; a stationary QB target stands several yards behind them. Use the coach's legal rush alignment. Start with at least several feet of free avoidance room on each edge. Volunteers keep hands at sides or behind backs and do not reach, shove, chase, or step late into Alina.

Do: walk the legal path twice. Then use 4 total reps, two each direction, for about 4–6 seconds each; rest 45–60 seconds. Sell one footwork fake and take the clear lane. Stop in balance near the target rather than crashing through. Initially use a stationary target or belt on a consenting stationary partner.

Progress: week 1 static screens; week 2 a helper signals the open edge before the approach; week 3 coach-supervised legal screen movement with room to avoid it. A blocker stepping into a collision is not a useful “toughness” drill. If the lane closes, contain and stop.

Score: legal/no contact, correct contain lane, balanced finish—one point each. Do not add extra tries to make up mistakes.

Contain-and-pull drill

Setup: a 5 × 7-yard channel, QB partner with the competition belt, Alina at a safe approach distance. QB jogs left or right on a cue, without throwing.

Do: stay on the assigned outside hip, angle the QB toward the team's help, shorten steps, and pull a flag without body contact. 4 reps, 45 seconds rest. Use this instead of the two-screen drill on a later week, not in addition. Progress only with the coach's approval.

Film question: did the double screen beat her first step, her chosen path, her second decision, or her finish? Work on the actual cause. If opponents are grabbing or initiating contact, bring specific plays to the coach rather than teaching Alina to fight through fouls.

Make it easy for the coach to trust her at receiver.

Good hands are the starting point. Consistent alignment, route depth, timing, separation without contact, and knowing the play make a stronger case than highlight catches. Learn two routes in the team's terminology before trying the entire route tree.

Reliable-hands block Easy skill day

Dose: 12–20 catchable throws from a comfortable 5–8 yards, not bullets at the face. Start stationary: 4 chest-height, 4 slightly left/right, 4 slightly low/high. If fresh, add 4–8 catches while walking or jogging.

Cues: find the ball early, present relaxed hands, watch it into the hands, absorb with the arms, and secure before turning. Hands adjust to the ball's height; do not force one hand shape for every catch. Use an appropriate ball and never bare-hand painfully hard throws.

Upgrade: progress the direction or timing, not both at once. Practice over-the-shoulder catches with a safe clear runout only after easy throws are reliable. Count catchable targets separately from bad throws.

Release and route landmarks

Setup: mark the coach-assigned start, break, and catch point. Begin without a defender. Use a small foot/shoulder fake, accelerate on the route stem, and make the break with controlled steps. No push-off or contact release.

Dose: 4 routes total on the easy skill day at walking/jogging pace. On optional B, use 4 total routes at the week's controlled pace, 45–60 seconds rest; these replace separate sprint reps. A partner can show passive coverage once spacing and control are reliable.

Cues: same initial look, correct depth, eyes at the right time, catch in balance. On a hitch, decelerate into the turn rather than skidding on locked legs. At the sideline, practice clearly inbounds catches without diving into traffic.

Scramble rules and after-catch choices

Ask how the coach wants receivers to adjust when the QB moves—do not randomly chase the ball or crowd another receiver. After a catch, tuck securely without blocking flag access, take an open lane, use a controlled juke if needed, and avoid a collision. Flag guarding under current NFHS emphasis requires contact that denies a flag pull; never use that distinction as a reason to train swats or stiff-arms. [R2]

Arrive balanced. See the flag. Finish cleanly.

The most common practice error to watch for is reaching from too far away at full speed. Alina needs to bring her feet with her hands. Angle pursuit toward the sideline or assigned help, watch the hips, take shorter steps near the runner, and reach for an exposed flag without grabbing clothing or wrapping the waist.

Fuel a growing athlete, not a smaller body.

No calorie cap, weigh-ins for appearance, cutting diet, fasting, or body-composition goal. Being double-teamed does not mean she needs to lose weight or “bulk.” Adequate food supports growth, school concentration, repeated sprints, recovery, and bone health. Portions need to reflect her appetite, growth, and workload. A sports dietitian can personalize needs without guesswork. [F1, H2]

Build full meals

  • Carbohydrate: rice, potatoes, pasta, oats, bread, tortillas, cereal, fruit. These support training; do not cut them out.
  • Protein: eggs, chicken, fish, beef, yogurt, milk, beans, lentils, tofu, or tempeh at regular meals and useful snacks.
  • Produce and fats: fruit/vegetables plus olive oil, avocado, nuts/seeds, or another tolerated fat.
  • Bone-supporting foods: milk, yogurt, cheese, or calcium/vitamin-D-fortified alternatives. Check labels; many plant drinks contain little protein.

Use flexible portions

Start with a normal family meal and eat until comfortably satisfied. On game/hard-practice days, add an extra carbohydrate serving and/or snack as needed. On rest days, keep meals and protein regular; recovery still needs energy.

Iron-containing foods matter: meat, beans/lentils, tofu, and fortified cereals; add vitamin-C foods alongside plant iron sources. Do not start iron or vitamin supplements without clinical advice. Protein powders, pre-workouts, fat burners, and energy drinks are not part of this plan. [F1]

Signs to bring to a clinician: persistent fatigue, dizziness, recurrent injuries or bone pain, declining performance, restrictive eating, or missed/markedly changed periods if she menstruates. Not eating enough for training can affect health and performance. These signs have multiple possible causes; they are not a diagnosis. Do not normalize menstrual changes as “just being an athlete.”

Food swaps without losing the fuel

For vegetarian meals, substitute beans/lentils/tofu/tempeh for meat while keeping the rice, pasta, or bread. For milk intolerance, use tolerated lactose-free dairy or a fortified alternative such as soy milk if suitable. Use sunflower-seed butter instead of nut butter when needed. Respect allergies, school restrictions, religious/cultural preferences, and food availability. If food restrictions are extensive, ask a registered dietitian to help make the plan complete.

Fall in Arizona still needs a heat plan.

October does not guarantee safe outdoor conditions. Use the school's athletic trainer and on-field wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) policy, not only the phone's air temperature. AIA Article 43 applies activity modifications to training, too. A shaded weather reading may not reflect a hot turf field. [H3, H4]

Simple hydration rhythm

  • Drink regularly with meals and snacks, and bring a bottle to school. Pale-yellow urine can be a rough cue, but is not a medical test or a reason to force fluids.
  • Start practice comfortably hydrated. A familiar pre-practice snack with roughly 1–2 cups of water is a reasonable example from sports-nutrition guidance; do not chug it all at kickoff. [F1]
  • Have fluids available and take regular breaks, roughly every 15–20 minutes or more often as the heat policy requires. Drink in comfortable amounts based on thirst and the trainer's individualized plan. There is no one hourly volume that is right for every teen.
  • Water is usually sufficient for short/light activity. For prolonged vigorous activity over an hour or substantial sweating, a sports drink with carbohydrate/electrolytes may help. Sports drinks are not energy drinks; avoid caffeine-heavy pre-workouts. [H4]
  • Afterward, drink gradually with a meal/snack and normal salty foods. Do not force gallons, take salt tablets, or use dehydration to change body weight. A trainer can individualize fluid replacement; routine at-home weigh-ins are not needed for this plan.

The published table leaves exact boundary values 82.0 and 92.0 unstated. For this optional plan, use the more restrictive band at those boundaries and follow the trainer's interpretation. Restrictions are ceilings, not permission to continue when anyone feels unwell. AIA calls for measurements on the playing surface about every 30 minutes or as conditions change.

Stop early: headache, nausea, cramps, dizziness, unusual weakness, or poor coordination mean stop, tell an adult, and get assessed in a cool place. Confusion, collapse, seizure, or loss of consciousness: call 911 immediately and begin rapid cooling under trained supervision. Heatstroke can occur while sweating. Do not wait for a thermometer reading or force oral fluids into someone confused or unconscious. Follow the site's emergency plan. [H3, H4]

After illness, a long break, or travel from a cooler climate, ask the trainer about gradually rebuilding heat exposure. Do not create extra hot-weather sessions to “get tougher.” Heat acclimatization does not make someone immune to heat illness.
