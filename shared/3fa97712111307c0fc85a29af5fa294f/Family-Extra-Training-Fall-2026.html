School practice is the main training.

Count everything: morning practice, games, PE, team lifting, other sports, and family drills. A free afternoon does not mean a recovered body. With two-hour school practices most weekdays, extra conditioning, hard cuts, maximum-speed sprints, and additional lifting are not the default.

Use this page for family extras instead of adding the older guide's Session A/B workouts. The training & fueling guide remains a drill and food reference; its larger sessions belong within or in place of coach-directed work. Do not stack the two plans.

These short routines are optional coaching examples, not a medical prescription or proof that more training is safe. The family's new practice-time information does not tell us the intensity, game calendar, injuries, or sleep she gets. Have her coach and athletic trainer approve whether any extras fit. Alina gets a say and may stop or skip without making up the work.

Before choosing a physical session

  • Feeling well: no pain, limping, illness, dizziness, headache after a hit, or unusual fatigue. Ongoing pain or declining performance needs the trainer/clinician, not a harder workout. [S1, S2]
  • Recovered and willing: normal energy, enough food and fluids, and adequate sleep. A hard morning, sore legs, a late night, school stress, or reluctance means skip the physical extra.
  • Calendar fits: no extras on game day. Within 24 hours before or after a game, use only optional brief film or playbook review if she wants. On congested weeks, omit physical extras altogether. These are conservative rules for this family plan, not universal recovery cutoffs.
  • Rest stays protected: aim for 1–2 days off organized sport each week, with at least one full day without team or family drills. Family-run drills count as structured training. If school commitments leave no full day off, omit extras and ask the coach/trainer to resolve the schedule. [S1, S2]
  • Safe setting: daylight or a properly lit, even field; enough clear space to stop; comfortable conditions; an adult present. No drills in the street, dark yard, cramped garage, or around furniture. Follow the school's heat and weather guidance.

Stop and get help: pain, limping, dizziness, unusual breathlessness, or symptoms after a head hit mean stop and tell an adult/trainer. No same-day return after suspected concussion. Chest pain, fainting, severe breathing trouble, or confusion/collapse in heat need urgent medical help; call 911 for severe or ongoing symptoms. Do not use the session chooser as medical clearance.

The 8–12-minute after-school extra.

Pick a daylight window after a snack and before dinner/homework pressure builds; the school dismissal and commute times are unknown. Do not delay sleep to fit it in. Keep effort easy enough to talk in full sentences, roughly 2–3 out of 10 effort; use walking or a comfortable jog rather than explosive cuts.

  1. 2–3 minutes — ease in. Walk, gently move ankles/shoulders, and make a couple of easy short tosses. Do not force stretches. The morning warm-up does not count as the afternoon warm-up.
  2. 4–6 minutes — choose ONE skill. Use easy hands, walking routes, or a walking rush read from the library. Stop at its rep cap or the time cap, whichever comes first. Rest 30–45 seconds between moving reps; spend the rest of the slot talking or resetting, not adding reps.
  3. 2–3 minutes — finish. Walk, drink as needed, and name one thing that felt cleaner. Put the ball away while she still feels fresh.

Example tonight: a parent throws 8–12 comfortable passes while Alina practices watching the ball into her hands and securing it before turning. A sibling can count catchable throws, collect balls only when play stops, or film one rep with permission. No route circuit, sprint finisher, or extra lifting afterward.

A 20–25-minute family field session.

Only use this on a suitable non-practice weekend day after the readiness check. A Friday evening game rules out Saturday morning; even after 24 hours, soreness, fatigue, or a demanding next practice can still rule it out. This is easy technique, not speed training. Ask the coach to place genuine sprint, hard-agility, and strength development within the school plan rather than adding it here.

Five drills the family can help with.

These are choices, not a circuit. Use her game ball, 2–4 flat markers, water, and an inspected legal competition belt when relevant. A willing adult can throw, cue, or wear flags; no special equipment purchase is needed. Confirm school-taught alignment and technique before introducing a change.

Easy hands & secure

Setup: a reliable thrower about 3–5 yards away, adjusted so every throw is gentle and comfortable. Clear space behind the receiver. Begin standing, feet balanced.

Do: 4 comfortable chest-height throws, 4 slightly left/right, and up to 4 with one small step before securing. 8–12 total throws; missed or poor throws do not trigger more repetitions.

Family cue: “See it into your hands, soften the catch, secure, then turn.” Count clean catches out of catchable throws without turning it into a contest. No bullets, dives, jumping catches, blindfolds, or balls aimed at the face.

Two familiar route landmarks

Setup: ask the coach for two routes she should know, including alignment and break/catch landmarks. Mark the path, leaving generous room to slow down. Without those instructions, use straight walking catches instead of inventing route depths.

Do: walk each route once without a ball, then once with a gentle throw: 4 total route reps. At most a relaxed jog on the last reps if the coach approves and she is fresh; no sharp cuts or timed race.

Family cue: “Line up, reach your landmark, look when the play calls for it.” Correct one detail, not her entire technique at once. A cone marks space; nobody plays physical coverage.

Read the open edge around two screens

Setup: two widely separated cones represent screeners, with a QB marker behind them. Use the coach's starting alignment and contain assignment. Keep enough room to walk around either side without squeezing between objects.

Do: a family member points to an available edge. Alina names her assignment, walks one small footwork fake toward the other side, then follows the clear path and stops balanced near the QB marker. 4 total reps, two each way, unless the coach's assignment permits only one side.

Family cue: “Find space; keep contain.” If an indicated route conflicts with the assignment, discuss rather than force it. Do not use adults as moving blockers, shove through a gap, or practice tackle-football hand moves. Review legal screening first.

Balanced flag reach

Setup: a consenting adult wears the inspected competition belt with flags exposed, stationary at first. Confirm the release works normally. Use the equipment required by the team; no makeshift tied flags.

Do: approach by walking, shorten steps, and reach for the flag without grabbing clothing or touching the body. Reset the belt after each attempt. 4 total attempts, two per side; a later session can use a straight walking partner if the coach has taught it.

Family cue: “Feet close, hips in view, flag only.” Stop after a miss rather than lunging. No wrapping, pushing, contact, or full-speed chasing; younger siblings should help as counters or camera operators, not live evasive runners. Follow the belt and flag-pull rules.

Ball-movement cue & first-step position

Setup: a family member holds the ball where Alina can see it; the coach's legal starting stance and alignment come first. Allow several clear yards around her.

Do: on actual ball movement, take just two controlled walking steps into the assigned lane and stop balanced. Reset fully. 4 total reps, substituting for a rush block rather than adding another drill.

Family cue: “Watch the ball, then move.” This rehearses attention and position; it is not a timed reaction test or a substitute for coach-supervised speed work. No anticipation contests, explosive starts, resisted sprints, weighted vests, or punishment for a false start.

Make the early morning easier.

Food around 5:30 a.m.

Prepare food and the school bag the night before. Use a small familiar carbohydrate snack on waking if tolerated, then a substantial carbohydrate-plus-protein breakfast after the 7:30 a.m. practice. Examples: banana or toast before; oatmeal with milk/fortified soy milk and eggs/tofu afterward. Her trainer or dietitian can help if early eating causes stomach problems.

Keep lunch, an after-school snack, and dinner. A family catch session is not a reason to skip food or add a supplement. See the full fueling guide and meal options.

Count backward from wake-up

Teens need 8–10 hours of sleep. [S3] If she must wake at 4:45 a.m., being asleep at 7:45 p.m. gives 9 hours; 8:45 p.m. gives only 8 hours. Those are sleep times, not the start of getting ready for bed. Her actual wake-up time may differ.

Wind down earlier and leave time to fall asleep. If homework, travel, or late games make adequate sleep impossible, drop extras and discuss the overall schedule with the family, coach, and trainer. A nap does not justify routinely shortening the night.

Heat, light, and family roles

Arizona afternoons can still be hot in fall. Follow the trainer's conditions-based heat plan; water, shade, and a short session do not make unsafe heat safe. Choose indoor discussion when outdoor conditions, air quality, lightning, or daylight are unsuitable. Review the heat and hydration guidance.

One adult manages the space and stops the drill when needed. Another person may throw or cue; rotate helpers, not extra reps for Alina. Ask permission before filming, give one calm cue, and let her say what she wants to work on. Encourage learning and enjoyment, not earning food, proving toughness, or winning playing time. [S2]

Progress without adding more minutes.

First week: try one short weekday extra after approval. Later weeks: if recovery stays normal and she enjoys it, keep the same rep limits and consider a second short slot or the optional weekend session. Change one skill emphasis rather than increasing speed, reps, and duration together.

Use a paper note if helpful: date, minutes, skill, one thing that improved, and how she felt the next morning. For catches, distinguish an uncatchable throw from a drop. For routes, look for the correct landmark. For rushing, check assignment and space. For flags, value balance and avoiding contact. No at-home body-weight tracking or timed sprint tests.

Cut back immediately if morning practice quality drops, she is persistently tired or sore, sleep suffers, enthusiasm disappears, or pain develops. Bring that pattern to the trainer/clinician. Do not wait for the end of a four-week block to respond.

A specific question for the coach: “She practices 5:30–7:30 most mornings. Is one short, easy family skill session appropriate right now? Which receiver route or rush decision should we reinforce, and which days should we leave completely alone?”
