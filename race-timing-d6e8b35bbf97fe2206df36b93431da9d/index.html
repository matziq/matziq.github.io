The short answerHow the same line works in both directions

Runners start down the plan and return up it. Because the tags are on the front of their bibs, the start and finish need antennas looking at them from opposite directions.

1. Before the startKeep two overhead panels facing the start and two facing the finish. Place both side stands where they face starting runners.
2. After the last starterIf the course is clear and the crew has enough time, move and re-aim the two side stands for the finish. The four overhead panels stay fixed.
3. Before runners returnTest the finish positions. Leave the reader running; use a pretested backup if a safe changeover is not possible.
Important: The proposed right-stand route needs a 25-ft low-loss extension or a longer replacement cable. The optional eight-panel upgrade also needs two additional antennas and cables. Measure the actual routes before buying or mounting.

Two more overhead panels may improve center coverage, but they also add switching time. The separate eight-antenna option explains the benefit, trade-off, connections, and the test for deciding whether it is worthwhile.

Keep stands, scaffold feet, and cables outside the clear runner opening. The venue and a qualified rigger must approve the structure and public clearance.

Distance: 5 km is 3.11 miles; 3.2 miles is about 5.15 km. Confirm the course distance with the race director.

After the last starterMove the two stands only if the course is safely clear
Changeover / once, after the last starter crosses

The overhead panels stay in place. The side stands are the only antennas to move. Set a conservative, event-specific cutoff before race day, based on the earliest plausible returning runner, crew clearance time, and a safety margin. Do not assume a fixed number of minutes after the gun.

Why add two antennas?Optional eight-panel upgrade, placement, costs, and trade-offs

Recommendation: Consider two additional MTI RHCP panels if the six-panel arrangement struggles with the center of the chute or dense packs. Mount one in the center of each overhead rail: one for the start and one for the finish. Keep them only if a realistic comparison shows that eight panels read more reliably than six.

Swipe sideways inside the diagram to see the whole layout.

What you gain

  • A stronger view of the middle: the two existing overhead beams already overlap there. A centered panel adds another viewing position, which may help when a bib is angled, partly covered, or in a weak spot.
  • More coverage in both directions: each direction has three overhead panels and the same two side stands. This is eight physical antennas total, not ten.
  • No additional race-day move: the two new panels remain fixed overhead. Only the two side stands may need to move.

The important trade-off

The R420 and hub switch between antenna paths; the panels do not all inventory tags at once. More paths can improve viewing angles but can also delay revisiting each antenna while runners are moving. As a simple equal-time illustration, six paths would each get 1/6 of the cycle and eight would each get 1/8: 25% less time per path. Actual scheduling and results vary. Eight panels are not automatically more reliable, and the views are not independent guarantees.

Decision rule: Compare six versus eight using the same representative bibs, lane positions, pack density, speeds, RF setup, and timing software. Choose eight only if it reduces misses or improves repeatable timing without making another condition worse. Record raw reads and verify every crossing with an independent count or video.

What to add

Buy two matching MTI RHCP panels, two compatible low-loss RF cables, and suitable overhead mounts. On the pictured scaffold, each new center-panel route is about 17 ft including a 3-ft allowance, so two 25-ft cables are plausible; measure the actual path and connector types first. The existing right-stand extension is still needed for the planned six- or eight-panel route. The hub's H1–H3 assignments remain unchanged; H4 and H5 add A5 and A6. Confirm logical IDs with the reader powered and a single test bib after installation.

Equipment station E (R420, hub, and GPIO adapter in a weather-protected box) mounts high on the left scaffold at the line, (x = -9, y = 0, z = 11 ft), outside the opening. Halfway between the rails, it keeps all four overhead runs within 25 ft. It also lets both stand cables come down at y = 0, so the stand move uses no extra cable.

The right stand needs a longer cable. The planned route goes over a rail, down the right scaffold, along the ground outside the barrier, and up the stand. It is about 53.5 ft including a 3-ft allowance in both stand positions. The supplied 40-ft cable is too short even before the stand moves. Add a compatible 25-ft low-loss extension, giving 65 ft total, or use one approximately 60–65 ft low-loss cable. Measure the installed route and connectors before purchasing.

Line width and crowd safetyA proposed 16-ft opening, subject to event approval

Starting proposal: 16 ft clear between barrier faces. Keep a straight, constant-width section through the read zone, with no equipment in the opening. Upstream of the start, taper gradually; the diagram's dimensions are an example, not a site plan. Do not force 1,200 runners into an abrupt bottleneck just to improve RFID reads. The race director and venue safety lead must approve the actual width, taper, crowd flow, escape routes, and any waves before the event.

Field testing and race-day checksTest both directions, the changeover, and six versus eight panels
  1. Count every crossing independently. Use video or observers and match each actual runner and crossing to the raw reads. Test both directions, left and right edges, the center, different heights and speeds, dense shoulder-to-shoulder packs, covered or wrinkled bibs, and representative wet conditions.
  2. Include realistic tag density. As safely practical, test a crowded approach and a large population of nearby bibs, not just small groups. Record false reads from waiting runners and after the finish.
  3. Compare six and eight. With the same people, tag types, course positions, density, power strategy, and timing software, repeat comparable passes using a six-path profile and an eight-path profile. Compare misses, time error against independently observed crossings, early reads, and measured antenna revisit intervals. Randomize test order when practical.
  4. Check cables and the changeover. Test each antenna individually, check the right-stand extension and connector loss, and rehearse both stands' full move without tension or a trip hazard. Verify the finish configuration before the event-specific cutoff.
  5. Use an initial acceptance target, not a guarantee. Aim for zero misses in at least 500 runner-passes, including 150 in dense start-direction packs, plus zero misses in 100 passes at 3 dB less power. Test both directions and the operating configuration. A small or correlated sample cannot prove perfect race-day capture; use an independent timing backup and resolve any misses before accepting the layout.
  6. Race morning: repeat representative left, center, and right passes in both directions, verify the reader clock and reports, and confirm the crowd-safety plan and backup recording.
