Two Churches, One Book?

A careful comparison of the Book of Mormon in The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints and Community of Christ: what the churches share, where the editions diverge, and why their interpretations grew apart.

Quick answer Church profiles Text comparison Timelines Did you know? Why they differ Sources

The short version

Same movement origins and a shared scripture, but not one identical modern edition or one identical theology.

✓ Shared / substantially the same

Common beginning: Both communities trace their religious roots to the movement Joseph Smith Jr. organized in 1830.

Common book: Both include the Book of Mormon in their scriptural tradition and inherit its same central narrative, characters, and Christ-centered teachings.

Much shared wording: The two traditions transmit a common textual lineage. Many passages are the same in substance and can be word-for-word identical; the entire pair of editions should not be treated as a single identical text.

↔ Different / sometimes vastly different

Edition and references: Their English print traditions diverged; chapter and verse systems do not line up neatly, and some readings, headings, and editorial material differ.

Scripture’s role: The LDS Church gives the Book of Mormon a central, distinctive role as another witness of Jesus Christ and a foundational Restoration scripture. Community of Christ also regards it as scripture, while explicitly framing the Bible as foundational and scripture as a human, contextual witness interpreted through Christ.

Doctrinal framework: The churches differ substantially on priesthood, temple practice, authority, and later revelations. Those differences arose through separate histories after 1844—not because one Book of Mormon was newly translated from scratch.

Precision matters: “Exactly the same” can mean identical wording in a particular passage, a shared story, or the same overall book. Those are different claims. The careful answer is: the traditions share a common Book of Mormon text and much identical wording, but they do not use identical editions, references, or interpretive frameworks throughout.

The two traditions today

Two church profiles

The names are related because both churches emerged from the nineteenth-century Latter Day Saint movement. They are separate denominations, with distinct leadership structures, canons, practices, and understandings of scripture.

The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints

Often called LDSHeadquartered in Salt Lake City

Book of Mormon in the church

One of four standard works, alongside the Bible, Doctrine and Covenants, and Pearl of Great Price. The church teaches that it is scripture and “another testament of Jesus Christ,” and gives it a major place in worship, missionary work, and personal study.

How the text is published

  • The current official English edition descends through several revisions from the 1830 publication; the 2013 edition made modest typographical and editorial updates to the 1981 text.
  • The 1879 Orson Pratt edition introduced the shorter chapter and verse arrangement that remains the familiar LDS reference system.
  • Official Church history describes textual revisions openly, including corrections made to spelling, grammar, punctuation, and some readings.

Historical path

After Joseph Smith Jr.’s death in 1844, Brigham Young led the largest group west; its church became The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. The LDS Church understands its own institutional authority and succession through that history.

Community of Christ

Formerly RLDSHeadquartered in Independence, Missouri

Book of Mormon in the church

Part of its scripture alongside the Bible and Doctrine and Covenants. Its official statement says the Bible is foundational; the Book of Mormon and Doctrine and Covenants are used not to replace the Bible but because they confirm its witness to Jesus Christ.

How the text is published

  • The RLDS/Community of Christ tradition developed its own editorial and publication line, including the historically important 1908 “Authorized Edition.”
  • Its inherited chapter arrangement follows the older 1830 structure more closely than the LDS layout created in 1879; verse references can therefore be substantially different.
  • The church encourages interpretation informed by faith, experience, tradition, scholarship, and the Holy Spirit; it does not define scriptural authority as inerrancy in every historical or scientific detail.

Historical path

After the 1844 succession crisis, a distinct Reorganization formed around Joseph Smith III, son of Joseph Smith Jr. It was formally organized in 1860 and later took the name Community of Christ in 2001. This is the Community of Christ’s own institutional history, not a neutral resolution of competing succession claims.

The actual Book of Mormon editions

Where are they exactly the same—and where not?

A useful comparison separates the shared literary work from each publisher’s particular edition. Chapter numbers, verse numbers, editorial apparatus, and text itself are related but not interchangeable.

Exactly the same in many passages

When the editions preserve the same wording in a passage, the words are literally the same—not just similar. Both churches’ texts share the same principal books, narrative arc, names, and core episodes. The underlying relationship is textual descent from the early Book of Mormon, not two unrelated books with the same title.

Same meaning; different address

The same sentence may be found under different chapter and verse numbers. The first 1830 printing had no verse numbers; later publishers divided and numbered the text differently. A citation copied across traditions can point to the wrong place unless the edition is checked.

Small editorial differences

Over time, printings corrected typographical slips and adjusted spelling, grammar, punctuation, wording, headings, and study aids. Some changes were deliberate clarifications; others were restoration of earlier manuscript readings or corrections of typesetting errors.

Vastly different: authority and theology

The most consequential contrast is often not a changed line of the Book of Mormon. It is what a church says the book is, how it should be read, and how it relates to the Bible, continuing revelation, church authority, and later doctrine. On those matters the traditions can differ profoundly.

What is not different

Community of Christ did not create a new Book of Mormon unrelated to the LDS one. Nor did the LDS Church acquire an entirely different ancient narrative after the split. Both inherit the same founding text and its early publication history.

What this page does not claim

It does not claim that every verse has been independently collated here or that every current printing contains precisely the same words. For a passage-level answer, compare the named editions side by side and account for the different reference systems.

Two histories, intertwined

How one movement became two traditions

The years below highlight the key turning points for the Book of Mormon and the churches’ separate editorial paths.

1827–1829 Translation period

Joseph Smith’s scribes produce the English text. Church history accounts report that nearly all of the present text was dictated between April and June 1829, principally with Oliver Cowdery as scribe.

1830 First printing

E. B. Grandin prints the Book of Mormon in Palmyra, New York. The large first run was 5,000 copies. The book had long chapters and no verse numbering; printer John Gilbert added punctuation and paragraph divisions.

1837 Second edition

Joseph Smith and Oliver Cowdery oversee more than a thousand corrections, mostly grammatical, along with some clarifications. The LDS Church’s official history details changes such as “mother of God” becoming “mother of the Son of God.”

1840 Nauvoo edition

The last edition supervised by Joseph Smith makes further corrections, including restoring “pure” in the “pure and delightsome” phrase. The text subsequently circulates through printings based on different editions.

1844 Succession crisis

Joseph Smith Jr. is killed in Illinois. Disagreement over leadership and authority leads to a succession crisis and multiple Latter Day Saint bodies; there was no single uncontested successor.

1847 Migration west

Brigham Young leads the largest body of Latter-day Saints to the Salt Lake Valley. The LDS Church’s later publishing history follows its western institutional line.

1860 RLDS organization

Joseph Smith III becomes president of the Reorganized Church of Jesus Christ of Latter Day Saints, formally organized in Illinois. This community preserves its own succession claim and remains centered in the Midwest.

1879 New LDS references

Orson Pratt’s LDS edition introduces short chapters and numbered verses that become the familiar LDS layout. This makes references unlike the older RLDS/Community of Christ chapter structure.

1908 RLDS edition

The RLDS church issues its historically important Authorized Edition. Its editorial tradition is distinct from the LDS 1879 arrangement and helps explain why citations between the two can be difficult to match.

1920 LDS study edition

A revised LDS presentation adds two-column formatting, chapter summaries, a pronunciation guide, and standardized book titles. Editorial apparatus increasingly shapes how readers navigate the book.

1981 & 2013 Modern LDS editions

The 1981 edition updates study helps and incorporates earlier corrections; the 2013 English edition makes further minor typographical and editorial adjustments and supports digital study.

2001 New name

The Reorganized Church adopts the name Community of Christ, reflecting a broader contemporary identity while retaining its roots in the Latter Day Saint movement.

The historical narrative is presented with each church’s own self-understanding distinguished from documented publication dates. “Succession” describes competing religious claims; the timeline does not decide which claim is theologically valid.

Small details, big context

Did you know?

A dozen quick facts about the book’s production and the histories of the two churches.

Did you know? • Printing

5,000 copies was a bold first run

The first printing was planned at 5,000—more than twice the average book run at the time, according to the LDS Church’s history of the publication.

Did you know? • Financing

Martin Harris mortgaged his farm

Harris provided the collateral that enabled E. B. Grandin to proceed with the printing. After sales disappointed locally, the mortgaged property was eventually sold.

Did you know? • Publishing

A printer supplied the punctuation

The manuscript sent to the printer had almost no punctuation. Compositor John Gilbert supplied punctuation and paragraph divisions while setting the type.

Did you know? • Lost pages

There were 116 manuscript pages lost

The earlier loss shaped how the later printing was guarded. Hyrum Smith reportedly delivered printer’s-manuscript pages to the shop in installments to protect them.

Did you know? • Manuscripts

One manuscript mostly survived; one did not

About 28% of the original manuscript survives. The printer’s manuscript, copied for the 1830 typesetting, survived nearly intact.

Did you know? • Early witness

The first edition printed two witness statements

The testimonies of the Three Witnesses and Eight Witnesses were included at the end of the 1830 edition. The witnesses describe different kinds of experiences with the plates.

Did you know? • Early editions

Joseph Smith personally revised editions

The 1837 edition was revised with Oliver Cowdery; the 1840 edition was the last Book of Mormon edition supervised by Joseph Smith.

Did you know? • Navigation

The first edition had no verse numbers

The numbering people use today is editorial scaffolding added long after the first publication. Later numbering systems are not all alike across church traditions.

Did you know? • Printing tech

Stereotype plates changed reprinting

For the 1840 edition, stereotype plates made repeated printings easier. This helped stabilize one version, even as other print lines continued from different editions.

Did you know? • Later scholarship

Textual criticism became a modern project

The Book of Mormon Critical Text Project, launched in 1988, compares manuscripts and editions to track variants and distinguish editorial changes from production errors.

Did you know? • Two editions

A phrase took two paths after 1840

In 2 Nephi 30:6, the 1908 RLDS edition and modern LDS editions differ: the former retained “white and delightsome,” while the LDS 1981 edition restored “pure and delightsome,” Joseph Smith’s 1840 revision. The 1908 committee explicitly treated its choice as a return to an earlier reading.

Did you know? • Community of Christ

“RLDS” became “Community of Christ” in 2001

The name changed more than a century after the 1860 organization. Older sources and publications may still use “Reorganized” or “RLDS.”

Did you know? • Succession

The split was not a single clean break

After 1844, many leaders and groups advanced different claims. Brigham Young’s followers formed the largest body; the Reorganization was organized later, in 1860, under Joseph Smith III.

The causes beneath the differences

Why did they become different?

There is no single cause. Separate institutional histories produced different editorship, numbering, canon, and theology. Each church’s approach to the book reflects what it believes scripture and religious authority are for.

1. The 1844 leadership crisis created separate institutions

Joseph Smith Jr.’s death left competing ideas about who could lead and how authority should pass. The LDS Church developed under Brigham Young and the westward migration; the Reorganization gathered around Joseph Smith III and was formally organized in 1860. Once the churches were institutionally separate, they produced and authorized their own editions and references.

Neither church’s claim about succession is a neutral historical fact; this page reports the outline and each body’s distinct trajectory without adjudicating religious legitimacy.

2. Printers and editors made real changes across editions

The 1830 typesetting introduced punctuation and paragraphing; the 1837 revision corrected many problems; the 1840 edition restored some readings; and later editions followed different source printings. Changes can be routine copyediting, restoration from a manuscript, a correction of an error, a modernization, or a doctrinally meaningful clarification. Those categories should not be collapsed into “the book was rewritten” or “nothing ever changed.”

3. The two publishers organized references differently

Orson Pratt’s 1879 LDS edition divided the text into shorter chapters and verses. The Community of Christ tradition retained an older chapter framework. A verse number is a locator created by editors, not part of the original manuscript. Similar text can therefore have different labels and be hard to compare by citation alone.

4. Their views of scripture and revelation developed in different directions

The LDS Church treats the Book of Mormon as a foundational standard work and a central witness of the Restoration. Community of Christ’s official scripture affirmations describe the Bible as foundational and the other scriptures as witnesses to Christ, interpreted responsibly through the church’s experience, tradition, scholarship, and the Spirit. The statement explicitly rejects inerrancy as a requirement for every historical or scientific detail.

Those are differing theological frameworks—not simply two opinions about punctuation.

5. A shared ancestor does not guarantee identical modern copies

Think of two branches of a book’s publication history. Both may preserve large, overlapping stretches from an ancestor text; each branch can then adopt different corrections, chapter breaks, verse numbers, headings, and study material. That is why “they have the same Book of Mormon” is broadly true about shared scripture and misleading if taken to mean every edition is a page-for-page or word-for-word match.
