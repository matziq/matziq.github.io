The plan

What you're actually going to learn

The whole page is 15 measures. You do not learn them in order.

Measures 1–4 contain the entire famous opening, and they loop — measure 3 is measure 1 again with a slightly busier left hand. So learning four measures gets you something that sounds finished and that you can play on repeat for as long as you like. Everything after that is decoration built on the same two chords.

Two facts make this far more approachable than it looks on the page:

  1. The right-hand melody uses only four keys. Your hand sits in one position and never moves.
  2. The left hand is two shapes that alternate. Learn shape A and shape B and you have the accompaniment.
Step 0

Get your bearings

Two minutes here saves hours later.

Black keys come in groups of two and three. That is your map. Middle C is the white key immediately to the left of any group of two.

This piece is in A major, which has three sharps. On page 1 there are no accidentals at all, which means one simple rule holds for the entire page:

Every F, C, and G you play is the black key just above it. F becomes F♯, C becomes C♯, G becomes G♯. No exceptions on this page. D, E, A and B stay white.
Step 1

The right hand — the tune everyone recognizes

Start here. It is the most rewarding five minutes in the whole project.

Set your hand once and leave it

Put your right thumb on D5 — that's the D one octave above middle C. Let the other four fingers fall naturally onto the next keys. Your hand now covers the entire melody.

You only need four of those five

The opening melody uses A5, G♯5, E5 and D5. Finger 3 (on F♯5) never plays — just let it rest there.

The melody, one measure at a time

Count out loud: "one and two and three and four and." Each note gets one syllable — they're all eighth notes, perfectly even.

Read the finger row on its own and you'll see the shape instantly: 5 4 5 4 5 2 5 1. Pinky and ring finger rocking back and forth, then a dip down, then a rock, then land on the thumb.

That last D5 is tied. Hold it through the next measure — three more slow beats — and don't re-press it. It's the resting point of the phrase.

The little run-up at the start

Before all of that there's a two-note pickup: a quick A4–C♯5 that leads into the first A5. It comes on the second half of the last beat — two fast notes, like a breath before the phrase.

Play those two notes, then hop the hand up into home position. That hop happens every time the phrase restarts, so practice it as its own little move.

Step 2

The left hand — the flowing part

This is what makes the piece sound like water. It's two shapes, alternating.

Shape A — the F♯ minor shape (beats 1–2)

Shape B — the D major shape (beats 3–4)

Don't try to stretch. Both shapes are wider than your hand — that's normal and intended. Let your hand travel to the right as you play the three notes, like a wave rolling. The sustain pedal holds the low note for you, so there is nothing to grip.

Step 3

Hands together — the training-wheels version

Do this before the real thing. It sounds like the song immediately and it teaches your hands to cooperate.

Left hand plays only the bottom note of each shape — F♯3 on beat 1, D3 on beat 3 — and just holds it. Right hand plays the full melody.

Play it slowly, four times through. When the coordination stops feeling like work, add the middle and top notes of the left-hand shapes back in.

Step 4

Hands together — the real thing

The anchor points are what matter. Everything else falls into place around them.

Both hands land exactly together on the count of 1 and the count of 3. Those two moments are your anchors. If they line up, the measure will sound right even if the notes in between are slightly uneven.

Step 5

The pedal

The score says "with Pedal," and it is not optional — it's what glues the sound together.

Right foot on the rightmost pedal, heel on the floor. The motion is simple and it repeats twice per measure:

  1. Press the pedal down as you play beat 1. Slightly after, not before.
  2. On beat 3, lift and press again in one quick motion. This clears the old chord and catches the new one.
The test: if it sounds muddy, you're changing too late. If you hear a gap, you're lifting too early. Practice the pedal with the left hand alone until the change is automatic — then add the right hand.

7–9A gentler, lower answering phrase. Left hand starts reaching further down the keyboard.

Schedule

A realistic four-week route

Fifteen to twenty focused minutes a day beats two hours on a Sunday. If a week takes you two weeks, that's completely normal.

Week 1

Right hand only, measures 1–4. Get the 5-4-5-4-5-2-5-1 shape into your fingers until you can play it without looking.

Goal: melody from memory, slowly.

Week 2

Left hand only. Shape A, then shape B, then alternating. Add the pedal to the left hand this week, not later.

Goal: left hand + pedal, eyes closed.

Week 3

Hands together — training-wheels version first, then the full left hand. Painfully slow. Use a metronome around ♩ = 40.

Goal: 4 measures, no stops.

Week 4

Push the tempo toward ♩ = 65. Add measure 3's fuller left hand. Start looping 1–4 as a performance.

Goal: it sounds like the recording.
Technique

Six things that make the difference

Practice slower than feels sensible
At half speed your brain has time to encode the movement. At full speed you're just rehearsing your mistakes. If you can't play it slowly and perfectly, you can't play it.
Count out loud, actually out loud
Silent counting drifts. Speaking "one and two and" forces the beat to stay honest, and it's the fastest fix for hands that won't line up.
Separate hands longer than you want to
The temptation is to rush to hands-together. Resist it. Each hand should be nearly automatic before they meet.
Drop, don't press
Let the weight of your relaxed arm fall into the key rather than pushing down with your fingers. This piece lives at a soft dynamic and tense fingers make it sound brittle.
Loop the seam, not the whole thing
When you stumble, it's almost always at a transition — the hop up to home position, or the shift into shape B. Practice just those two beats, ten times, then put them back in context.
"Freely" means you're allowed to breathe
The tempo marking gives you permission to stretch the long notes and ease into phrases. Learn it strictly with a metronome first — then let it flex. Rubato is what you do after you own the rhythm.
Fixes

When something goes wrong

SymptomWhat's actually happening
Hands drift apartYou're watching your hands instead of counting. Say the counts out loud and make beats 1 and 3 land together — ignore everything in between for now.
Left hand can't reachYou're trying to hold the shape as a chord. Don't. Let the hand travel sideways and let the pedal hold the low note.
It sounds muddyPedal isn't changing on beat 3, or isn't lifting fully. Practice the lift-and-press motion away from the keys until it's a reflex.
Melody sounds lumpyUneven eighth notes. Slow to half tempo with a metronome and make every note the same length before speeding up.
You keep losing your placeYou're reading note-by-note instead of by shape. Memorize the four measures — there's less material here than it looks.
Wrong-sounding notesAlmost certainly a missed sharp. Every F, C and G on this page is a black key.
