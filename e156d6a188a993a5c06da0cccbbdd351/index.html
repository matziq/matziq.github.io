Zurich, 20 days after hip replacement

A practical guide to the medical tradeoffs, the long-haul flight, mobility support in Zurich, a low-demand sightseeing day, and the questions that should determine whether this trip goes forward.

Plain-language conclusion: postponing is the safer option. A same-day discharge does not make hip replacement minor surgery, and a Phoenix-to-Zurich trip on postoperative day 20 combines two independent clot risks: recent major surgery and prolonged travel. Her own surgeon’s “do not recommend” advice should carry more weight than general online guidance or another patient’s good outcome.

1

The bottom line

Why the answer is more cautious than “she may feel pretty good by week three.”

Primary concern

Clot and pulmonary embolism

The CDC lists both travel longer than four hours and surgery within three months as risk factors. A clot can occur with no leg symptoms before becoming a pulmonary embolism.

Very likely

Swelling, pain, and fatigue

Even an uncomplicated recovery commonly includes leg swelling, poor sleep, stiffness, and a sharply limited energy budget. Airports and transfers are often harder than the destination.

Low frequency, high impact

Wound, fall, or dislocation problem

A wet or infected incision, sudden twist, fall, or hip instability abroad can turn a two-day visit into urgent imaging, hospitalization, and a difficult insurance claim.

Why recommendations differ

AAHKS says many people can travel within a few weeks if individually assessed and protected. Some NHS orthopedic guidance advises waiting three months before long-haul travel. A retrospective study found no higher symptomatic clot rate among 220 patients who flew home soon after joint replacement, but it was observational and does not prove that an intercontinental itinerary is safe. The evidence is limited enough that individualized surgical advice wins.

2

Likely issues, organized by phase

The challenge is the entire door-to-door journey, not only the hours in the air.

At home during the first three weeks

Common and usually manageable

  • Thigh, groin, buttock, or incision pain
  • Leg and ankle swelling that worsens later in the day
  • Sleep disruption and pronounced fatigue
  • Constipation or nausea from pain medication
  • Need for a walker or cane; slower transfers and stairs
  • Difficulty with socks, shoes, low seats, and bathrooms

Recovery can change quickly

  • A strong day can be followed by a painful, swollen day.
  • Overdoing walking often shows up later, not immediately.
  • Changing from walker to cane is a clinical progression, not a travel deadline.
  • The approach-specific hip precautions may still apply.
During the airports and flights

Medical and physical issues

  • Long sitting increases venous stasis and swelling.
  • Economy seats can make safe hip positioning difficult.
  • Standing to walk may be unsafe during turbulence.
  • Bathroom transfers are cramped and may require twisting.
  • Dehydration, sedatives, or alcohol can reduce mobility.

Operational issues

  • Wheelchair waits, remote gates, jet bridges, and connections
  • Carrying or lifting luggage despite restrictions
  • Medication schedules crossing nine time zones
  • Delayed or canceled flights extending the travel day
  • Airline refusal if she appears medically unfit to travel
In Zurich and on the return
  • Cobbles, curbs, wet pavement, tram tracks, and hills increase fall and fatigue risk.
  • Jet lag may worsen balance, pain tolerance, medication timing, and sleep.
  • One active day can trigger swelling just before the return flight.
  • Anticoagulants can make a fall or wound bleeding more consequential.
  • A new problem must be evaluated by clinicians without the operative record or surgeon.
  • Travel medical insurance may exclude a foreseeable complication or travel against medical advice.

Emergency symptoms

Call emergency services immediately for sudden shortness of breath, chest pain, coughing blood, fainting, or a fast/irregular heartbeat. Seek urgent clinical advice for new one-sided calf or thigh swelling, unexplained tenderness, warmth, or redness; fever; increasing wound redness or drainage; sudden severe hip/groin pain; a “pop”; leg shortening/rotation; or inability to bear weight.

Decision framework and options

Treat “not recommended” as a meaningful medical warning, not a soft approval.

OptionMedical tradeoffPractical tradeoff
Postpone both travelers Safest; allows wound review, mobility progression, and lower postoperative risk. Change costs, but likely a much better trip. Ask airlines/hotels now about medical waivers or credits.
You go; she stays home Avoids her travel exposure, but only if she has reliable help at home and the surgeon agrees she can be without you. Preserves the purpose of a time-sensitive trip while requiring backup caregiving, transport, and emergency contacts.
Move only her ticket Same benefit as staying home; she joins on a later, cleared trip. Depends on fare rules and whether the event can be separated.
Travel as planned with clearance Highest-risk option. Risk can be reduced, not removed. Requires wheelchair assistance, low-demand itinerary, upgraded seat if possible, insurance confirmation, and willingness to cancel at the last minute.

A sensible decision gate

Do not make a final “go” decision today. Keep cancellation options open and schedule a specific pre-travel review close to departure. Proceed only if the surgeon changes from “do not recommend” to an affirmative, individualized clearance after examining her recovery—and gives a written clot-prevention and medication plan.

4

Questions to ask the surgeon

Ask for clear, documented answers rather than a general “use your judgment.”

Risk and clearance

  1. “Do you consider her medically fit for this specific Phoenix–Zurich round trip on postoperative days 20–23?”
  2. “What patient-specific factors make you advise against it?”
  3. “Would your answer change after a wound check or if we used lie-flat seating and wheelchair service?”
  4. “What finding would make this an absolute no-go?”

Clot prevention

  1. “What anticoagulant and exact timing should she use across the travel days and time-zone change?”
  2. “Should she wear graduated compression stockings? Which pressure, size, and duration?”
  3. “How often should she move, and is aisle walking safe for her?”
  4. “Are there reasons she should not use compression?”

Movement and equipment

  1. “Which hip precautions still apply based on her surgical approach?”
  2. “Walker, two crutches, cane, or wheelchair—which should travel with us?”
  3. “Can she sit for the expected flight duration, use a low aircraft toilet, and safely transfer?”
  4. “What is her lifting limit, and may she handle any luggage?”

Contingency plan

  1. “Which wound, swelling, pain, fever, or breathing symptoms mean cancel or seek emergency care?”
  2. “Can you provide an operative summary, implant information, medication list, and contact number?”
  3. “If a Zurich clinician needs advice, how can they reach the surgical team?”
  4. “When is the safest realistic date for this long-haul trip?”
5

If she travels despite the elevated risk

Only after explicit clearance; these measures reduce risk but do not make the trip “safe.”

Before booking or changing tickets
  • Request wheelchair assistance for every airport and connection through the airline; use it even if she can walk short distances.
  • Choose an aisle seat with maximum legroom. A lie-flat business seat may improve positioning but does not eliminate clot risk.
  • Avoid a tight connection; allow generous transfer time and minimize terminal changes.
  • Call the airline’s medical desk about recent major surgery and any fit-to-fly form.
  • Confirm travel medical coverage, postoperative exclusions, “travel against medical advice” language, and medical evacuation in writing.
  • Prearrange a wheelchair at the hotel rather than assuming same-day availability.
Medication and documents
  • Carry all medication in original labeled containers in hand luggage, plus extra for delays.
  • Get a written schedule showing when to take each drug in Phoenix time, in flight, and in Zurich time.
  • Carry the discharge summary, operative note or short surgical summary, implant information, allergies, medication list, surgeon contact, and insurance contacts.
  • Bring prescribed dressings and only surgeon-approved ice/cold-pack options.
  • Do not add aspirin or alter anticoagulant dosing for the flight unless the surgeon specifically directs it.
On the travel day and in the cabin
  • You handle all bags. She should not lift, twist, or rush.
  • Preboard, keep the mobility aid available to the aircraft door, and tell cabin crew she recently had hip surgery.
  • Follow the surgeon’s movement schedule. The CDC advises frequent leg movement and calf exercises; AAHKS suggests walking or stretching every one to two hours when safe.
  • Perform frequent ankle pumps and calf contractions while seated.
  • Hydrate steadily. Avoid alcohol and avoid sedating sleep aids unless the surgeon explicitly approves them.
  • Use prescribed compression exactly as instructed; incorrect size or use is not harmless.
  • Keep the aisle clear and shoes secure to reduce falls. Never walk during turbulence.
  • If she cannot safely use the aircraft bathroom, the itinerary is not practically viable.
At the hotel
  • Request step-free entry, elevator access, walk-in shower, grab bars, firm/high chair, and a bed height she can use safely.
  • Avoid a deep bathtub, low sofa, or low toilet unless approved equipment is provided.
  • Ask for a room near the elevator but away from long corridors.
  • Build in a recovery block after arrival and before the return flight. Elevate and ice only as instructed.
  • Use taxis rather than making transit access part of the challenge.

Zurich assistance options

Book support before departure; do not plan to improvise after landing.

Airport

Zurich Airport assistance

Request reduced-mobility assistance through the airline at least 48 hours before departure. Registered arriving passengers receive assistance from the aircraft; airport standards target no more than 20 minutes for all pre-registered arrivals.

Official ZRH guidance

City movement

Taxi + wheelchair

For this recovery window, a prearranged taxi and folding wheelchair are simpler than managing trams, platforms, crowds, and cobbles. Ask the hotel to confirm a wheelchair-accessible vehicle if she cannot transfer into a standard car.

Equipment

Mobility rental

Commercial providers advertise wheelchair and walker delivery in Zurich. Confirm dimensions, delivery window, deposit, and hotel acceptance before paying. Bringing a familiar aid from home is often more reliable.

A realistic one-day Zurich plan

One anchor activity, seated views, taxis, and a built-in exit—not a walking itinerary.

Choose based on how she feels that morning

The goal is not to “get your money’s worth.” If swelling, pain, dizziness, fatigue, wound concerns, or unsafe transfers are worse, convert the day to hotel rest and a short scenic taxi ride—or cancel sightseeing entirely.

09:30–10:30

Slow start at the hotel

Breakfast, medications, wound check, and a mobility check. No early alarm after an intercontinental arrival.

10:30–11:00

Taxi to Bürkliplatz

Skip the walk down Bahnhofstrasse. Confirm the exact boarding point, vessel, weather, and accessibility before leaving the hotel.

11:00–12:30

Short Lake Zurich cruise

A lake cruise offers high sightseeing value while seated. ZSG says wheelchair access varies by vessel/route; most main-deck areas are accessible, but the wheelchair width limit is 80 cm and some boats or river stops are exceptions. Verify the specific sailing.

12:45–14:15

Reserved, step-free lunch

Choose a restaurant close to the dock with a firm chair, accessible restroom, and no stairs. Reserve and confirm—not just “request”—accessibility.

14:15 decision point

Return to hotel or one indoor stop

Default: taxi back to rest, elevate, and protect the return flight. Optional only if she is doing very well: one accessible museum for 45–60 minutes using the wheelchair.

Evening

Hotel or nearby dinner

No cobbled Old Town loop. Arrange room service or a restaurant within a short, flat transfer. Pack early so the next travel day remains low demand.

What to skip on this trip
  • Uetliberg, long station transfers, and any “panoramic walk”
  • Lindenhof climbs, long Old Town cobblestone routes, and tram-hopping
  • A full Bahnhofstrasse walk, standing tours, or multiple museums
  • Long lake cruises that make it difficult to exit if pain or nausea rises
  • Anything with a nonrefundable timed entry that encourages pushing through symptoms
Bad-weather alternative

Use a prebooked taxi for a short city-and-lakeside driving loop, followed by a reserved accessible lunch and return to the hotel. Wet pavement and tram tracks are unnecessary fall risks. If choosing a museum, verify step-free entry, wheelchair availability, seating, elevators, and accessible toilets directly with the venue.

What other patients commonly suggest

Useful operational wisdom, but anecdotes cannot predict her outcome.

“Recovery is not linear.”

Patients describe “two steps forward, one step back.” Feeling strong at two weeks does not guarantee the same function after a long travel day at three weeks.

“Use the aid you were given.”

People often regret dropping the second crutch, walker, or cane too soon and developing a limp or overloading tissues. Travel is not the time to prove independence.

“The airport is the hidden activity.”

Experienced travelers recommend wheelchair service even when walking is possible. It preserves energy, limits rushing, and reduces fall exposure.

“Do less than you think you can.”

Swelling and pain often arrive hours after overexertion. Patients advise one meaningful outing, planned sitting, and an easy return route.

“Be completely candid with insurance.”

Forum contributors repeatedly warn that recent surgery must be disclosed. Get answers in writing so there is no ambiguity if a claim occurs.

“Do not push through sharp or new pain.”

Normal soreness and fatigue are not the same as sudden groin pain, instability, a pop, or loss of weight-bearing. Those require clinical assessment.

These themes were synthesized from patient discussion, including the Rick Steves Travel Forum. They are not clinical evidence, and the page contains a range of recovery timelines and opinions.

Emergency and cancellation plan

Decide in advance what happens if the answer becomes “no” at any point.

Cancel and contact the surgical team

  • Fever or chills
  • Wound opening, new drainage, spreading redness, or increasing warmth
  • New or worsening one-sided calf/thigh swelling or pain
  • Uncontrolled pain, dizziness, faintness, or unsafe walking/transfers
  • Any surgeon-defined no-go finding

Emergency now

  • Sudden trouble breathing or chest pain
  • Coughing blood, fainting, or a very fast/irregular heartbeat
  • Sudden severe hip pain, deformity, inability to bear weight, or major fall
  • Uncontrolled bleeding while anticoagulated

United States: 911 • Switzerland: 144

Do not board hoping she will improve in the air

If concerning symptoms appear at PHX, at a connection, or at ZRH, stop the trip and seek assessment. Cabin care is limited, diversion is complex, and the return journey may be medically impossible after a complication.

Action checklists

Use these as discussion prompts, not substitutes for clinical instructions.

Do now

  • Ask airline and hotel about medical change/cancellation options
  • Ask insurer about recent-surgery and against-medical-advice exclusions
  • Tell surgeon the exact dates and approximate flight duration
  • Plan backup care if she stays home
  • Request refundable or movable arrangements

If conditionally proceeding

  • Book a pre-travel surgical review
  • Obtain written clearance and prevention plan
  • Request wheelchair help for every flight segment
  • Select aisle/extra-legroom or lie-flat seat
  • Reserve accessible hotel room and taxi
  • Verify a suitable lake sailing and wheelchair dimensions

Pack in hand luggage

  • Original medication containers and written timing plan
  • Operative/discharge summary and implant details
  • Surgeon contact and insurance/assistance numbers
  • Prescribed compression garments and wound supplies
  • Mobility aid; do not check the only required aid
  • Loose clothing and stable, slip-resistant shoes

Final go/no-go

  • Surgeon affirmatively clears this specific itinerary
  • Wound status is acceptable to the surgical team
  • Pain and medication effects are stable and manageable
  • Transfers and bathroom use are safe
  • No clot, infection, breathing, or instability warning signs
  • You are both willing to cancel without debate
